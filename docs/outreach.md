# Outreach: messages that ask someone for something

Job applications, proposals, cold DMs, pitches, intro emails. Anything where the
reader can say no by doing nothing.

Read this before drafting one. The failure is not bad writing. The failure is
writing something that is easy to ignore.

## The two rules that are broken most often

Both of these were broken by an agent draft on 2026-09-06, on four messages in a
row, and neither was obvious until the user brought the evidence.

**1. Link the one relevant piece, not the whole portfolio.** A homepage asks the
reader to do the picking, and a busy reader will not. Send the single case study
that matches their world, and offer the rest as a second link. Same for a CV:
name the one engagement that maps to their brief.

**2. End with a question they can answer in one line.** A message with no
question needs no reply. A good question is specific to their situation, cheap to
answer, and changes what you would send next. It gives a reader who is on the
fence a way to engage that costs them nothing.

The best question does double duty by probing the blocker. For a US-only remote
role: *"Is the US-based requirement about payroll, or about hours overlap?"* That
lets them answer "payroll, sorry" or "hours, let's talk" instead of not replying.

## What gets deleted

Attributed, not verified: a client's public account of rejecting 1000+ proposals
on Upwork (Vadym Ovcharenko, LinkedIn, 2026-09). His claim is that 95% are
deleted in under ten seconds. Treat the percentage as his figure and the patterns
as the useful part, since they match what any inbox owner will tell you.

1. **The same template sent to fifty jobs.** No detail from their post, no
   research about them.
2. **The resume dump.** Whole life story, every skill ever learned, nothing about
   their specific problem.
3. **The undercut.** Leading on being cheaper, no mention of value or result.
4. **The generalist.** "I can do anything you need." No named strength.
5. **The red flags.** Typos, impossible timelines, no proof of work.

## What earns a reply

- Show you read their post, by naming something only a reader would know.
- One relevant case study, not the portfolio.
- An intelligent question about their project.
- Expertise in a niche, said plainly.
- Short. They are busy.

## The exception: a form is not a proposal

An application form, an agency's candidate database, a screening questionnaire.
Nobody is choosing between fifty of them side by side, and there is no human to
ask a question. Different rules apply:

- **Fill every field.** A blank qualification or an empty date range drops the
  record out of searches it would have passed.
- **Never leave a default sitting in a box.** A rate field that ships at 1
  becomes a $1/hour bid. Check every numeric field before submitting.
- **Spend the character limit on what they filter by.** Seniority, location,
  availability, two recognisable clients. Not narrative.

Applying proposal tactics to a form wastes the space. Applying form thinking to a
proposal makes it deletable.

## Register

Short sentences. One idea per sentence. A concrete picture instead of the jargon
word. No nested abstract clauses: they are the house style of generated text and
they read as AI even when the content is true. The aim is prose that reads as
approachable rather than generated.

Every claim in an outreach message must be one you can defend if asked. If a win
is your account rather than a documented fact, either leave it out or say it as
memory. One unbackable claim costs more than it earns.
